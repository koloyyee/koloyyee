## The Designers, Processors, and Inspectors.

When I look at a business process, I often categorize the different departments into designers, processors, and inspectors. They can be a single department or nested inside different departments.

By designers, I mean those whose role is client-facing: identifying the problem, finding optimal solutions, and planning for the future. Management, product design, business/data analytics, even Chef de Cuisine have these responsibilities.

Inspectors are quality controllers. They ensure the products and services comply with requirements, rules and regulations. Departments include compliance, quality assurance, legal, and accounting. Their job is reactive, and many consequences if they fail their duties.

Lastly, processors. They are the implementation team. They realize the design and comply with the inspectors' requirements. They are the builders, often measured by how quickly and accurately they can deliver the product. Software developers, builders, cooks, data analysts, photographers, etc. are the processors. Of course, they also design the solutions, but that’s not their main focus; the speed and efficiency of delivering are what they are worth.

Then why are processors the most vulnerable? Because they are the most valuable at times when skilled labour is short. Once the technology is on par with or surpasses what they can achieve those repetitive tasks faster, the value of the labour would be reduced.

## Automating the Process
When we look at automation, including AI and robotics, they are a threat to the processor ecosystem; when we look at the car and garment industry, there was a lot of pushback on automation. And when we look at coding, there is similar pushback. History has rhymed.

Skilled labourers are not going away; it only makes it harder to join, because the bar is much higher. Personally speaking, I haven’t jumped on this bandwagon, and I can feel it is getting even harder.

## Entry-levels are doomed?
So what should entry-level processors, like myself, do? Ask yourself, “If I want to be a processor?” There is not a job for everyone; some people have more talent than others. If you do want to be a processor, I think the best we can do is to be the first inspector in the implementation process by building a solid foundation of knowledge.

Entry-level processors need to have the ability to implement and understand that code. Identifying time and space complexity is also crucial. Processors will need to be the guardrails of the AI before it wastes time and tokens.

They also need to have the ability to “pop the hood open” and fix the machine like a car mechanic. That's why knowing how to read the code is getting even more important.

## Know a little more
Processors need to learn about communication. From talking to clients or designers to LLM models, the processors are no longer a siloed craftsman living in the basement. We have to face the world and give better and more accurate commands to the AI models, and reduce time and token waste.

How about the designers and inspectors? They also won’t go away, because for the design team, they are dealing with humans. Humans are the most volatile factor; products and services are here to solve human problems.

For the inspection team, they are also reactive, but they bear the risk for the firm; laws and regulations are forever changing. Moreover, not a single AI model company is willing to take the responsibility for millions of businesses; only humans can take up the responsibility.

If history doesn’t repeat itself but it rhymes, then processors must remember to pick up the design thinking or inspection trait even more. Because humans can hardly be faster than machines, if you find yourself focused on speed on repetitive tasks and often ignoring human interaction. You are in danger. It is the human side of the business that defines the value of the business, which ultimately shows the worth of an employee.

For processors with experience and domain knowledge in technical terms or the industry, it will be the moat. People often underestimate how much we can store and how quickly we can retrieve information.

## AI and future of labour
I doubt AI will completely replace human labour. 1) LLMs are not free and never will be; either compute, electricity, or cooling are the base cost for self-hosted models, or token cost from on-premises or cloud-hosted models. 2) The coupling risk is high; if the business solely relies on LLMs, the service quality and cost will be in the hands of a few major players, as we can see in the cloud business. The difference is that cloud companies are providing infrastructure support for the whole business, not a slice.

I believe AI will coexist with humans. Just like any tool, it should accelerate rather than replace.

Now, I have to ask myself, is it worth going through the hardship to do something I might be very good at and not very interested in? Absolutely not. I worked in the kitchen for over 10 years; I know I am not a great processor; therefore, I would rather focus on the design because I am better at analyzing. What about you?
